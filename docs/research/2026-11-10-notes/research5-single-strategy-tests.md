## 1. Sharpe ratio inference: Lo 2002, PSR, MinTRL, DSR

**Lo (2002)**
- (b) Andrew W. Lo, "The Statistics of Sharpe Ratios", Financial Analysts Journal 58(4), July/August 2002, pp. 36-52. [page] Free PDF read: https://traders.studentorg.berkeley.edu/papers/The-Statistics-of-Sharpe-Ratios.pdf . Journal page: https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2002/the-statistics-of-sharpe-ratios [snippet]
- (a) IID standard error: SE(SR_hat) = sqrt( (1 + 0.5*SR^2) / T ), estimated by plugging in SR_hat. 95% CI = SR_hat +/- 1.96 * SE. [page] (same PDF, Eq. 9-10)
- Example in paper: with T = 60 observations, SE = 0.188 when true SR = 1.50 and 0.303 when true SR = 3.00; a bigger SR has a bigger SE in absolute terms. [page]
- SE as a share of SR tends to a finite limit (1/sqrt(2T)) as SR grows. [page]
- (c) Assumes IID returns for Eq. 9. For non-IID (stationary) returns, the SE comes from a GMM / Newey-West estimate of the asymptotic variance, SE = sqrt(V_GMM / T). [page]
- Time aggregation: multiply a per-period SR by sqrt(q) only if returns are IID. For stationary returns, SR(q) = eta(q) * SR, where eta(q) = q / sqrt( q + 2 * sum_{k=1..q-1} (q-k) * rho_k ), rho_k = k-th autocorrelation. [page]
- For AR(1) returns with coefficient rho, positive serial correlation lowers the scale factor below sqrt(q) and negative serial correlation raises it. [page]
- Numbers from the paper: monthly SR to annual factor is 3.46 under IID and 2.88 when monthly first-order autocorrelation is +20%. With -20% monthly autocorrelation the annual SR is 4.17 times the monthly SR. [page]
- Empirical example: using 10 mutual funds, robust (Newey-West style, m = 3) SEs of annual SR differ from the IID SE; the paper says serial correlation can overstate a hedge-fund annual SR by up to about 65 percent. The 65% figure is from the paper's abstract-level summary [snippet via search result]; the example tables [page] were only partly read.
- Rule of thumb stated by the source itself: none numeric; the paper warns the sqrt(q) rule "should not be taken at face value" for longer horizons and that SR alone should not be the sole criterion. [page]
- Note: Mertens (2002) commented on the IID variance in Lo's paper; the skew/kurtosis version of the SE is attributed to Mertens and Christie (see below). Mertens page: https://www.elmarmertens.com/research/workingpapers [unconfirmed: not opened; cited via portfoliooptimizer.io].

**SE of SR under non-normal returns (Mertens 2002 / Christie 2005 / Opdyke 2007)**
- (a) Var(SR_hat) ~ (1 - g3*SR + ((g4 - 1)/4) * SR^2) / T. For normal returns g3 = 0, g4 = 3, which gives (1 + 0.5*SR^2)/T, the same as Lo's IID result. [page: portfoliooptimizer.io formula; the normal-case reduction is my arithmetic check]
- Source: https://portfoliooptimizer.io/blog/the-probabilistic-sharpe-ratio-bias-adjustment-confidence-intervals-hypothesis-testing-and-minimum-track-record-length/ [page]. It cites Opdyke, J.D. (2007), "Comparing Sharpe ratios: So where are the p-values?", Journal of Asset Management 8, 308-336, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/palgrave.jam.2250084 (not opened) [snippet].
- Small-sample bias stated on that page: E[SR_hat] = SR * (1 + (g4 - 1) / (4T)). [page]
- Assumptions stated on that page: returns stationary and ergodic; results asymptotic; sigma computed with T (not T-1). Sharpe ratios are comparable only if computed on the same number of observations. [page]

**Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio (PSR) and Minimum Track Record Length (MinTRL)**
- (b) David H. Bailey and Marcos Lopez de Prado, "The Sharpe Ratio Efficient Frontier", Journal of Risk 15(2), Winter 2012/13, pp. 3-44. SSRN: https://ssrn.com/abstract=1821643 (SSRN page not opened; abstract seen via https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:rsk:journ4:2223785 [snippet]). Direct author PDF (davidhbailey.com) failed with TLS error on fetch. Slides: https://newyork.qwafafew.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2017/01/Lopez_de_Prado_Sharpe.pdf [unconfirmed: not opened].
- (a) PSR(SR*) = Phi( (SR_hat - SR*) * sqrt(T - 1) / sqrt(1 - g3*SR_hat + ((g4 - 1)/4)*SR_hat^2) ). Reading: probability the true SR exceeds benchmark SR*. [snippet: formula from a fetch-tool summary of the PDF that did not render; consistent with the page below]
- Same family on portfoliooptimizer page: PSR(c) = Phi( (SR_hat - c) / SE_hat ); equals one minus the p-value of a right-tailed test of H0: SR <= c. [page]
- MinTRL(c) = (1 - g3*SR_hat + ((g4 - 1)/4)*SR_hat^2) * ( z_(1-alpha) / (SR_hat - c) )^2. (One source adds "1 +" in front; the portfoliooptimizer page omits it, the fetch-tool summary of the PDF includes it.) [page for the version without 1+; snippet for the version with 1+]
- (c) Assumes the return process keeps the same statistical properties out of sample (R PerformanceAnalytics doc). [page: https://rdrr.io/cran/PerformanceAnalytics/man/MinTrackRecord.html] That doc also warns that higher moments need lots of data and kurtosis should not be used for short series. [page]
- (d) Stated thresholds: alpha = 0.05, i.e. 95% confidence; PSR above 0.95 counts as significant. [page: portfoliooptimizer for alpha=0.05; the "PSR > 0.95" wording is [snippet]]. c = 0 tests for any skill; c = 1 (annualised) tests return relative to volatility. [page]
- Worked examples on that page: a Bitcoin RSI strategy with 136 monthly observations and annualised SR about 1 needed MinTRL of about 184 months to show at 95% that true annual SR exceeds 0.75 (about 48 more months). [page]
- (e) Python: jsharpe (PyPI 0.6.3, 2026-07-09, MIT; https://github.com/tschm/jsharpe ) functions `probabilistic_sharpe_ratio(SR, SR0, T, gamma3, gamma4)`, `sharpe_ratio_variance`, `minimum_track_record_length(SR, SR0, alpha)`, `critical_sharpe_ratio`, `sharpe_ratio_power`, `control_for_FDR`, `adjusted_p_values_bonferroni/holm/sidak`. No deflated function listed on the README. [page]
- Other Python: sharpebench (PyPI 0.28.0, 2026-09-18, MIT OR Apache-2.0) advertises deflated Sharpe, PSR, PBO, Reality Check, SPA, BH-FDR; PyPI page failed to load, function names `probabilistic_sharpe_ratio`, `deflated_sharpe_ratio`, `min_track_record_length` come from a search summary [snippet]. deflated-sharpe (PyPI 0.1.0, 2026-03-21, Apache-2.0; https://github.com/mnemox-ai/deflated-sharpe ) has `deflated_sharpe_ratio` and `min_backtest_length` [snippet; PyPI metadata read, API not opened]. backtrader.analyzers.sharpe_ratio_stats has PSR, MinTRL, DSR functions [snippet]. R: PerformanceAnalytics `MinTrackRecord`, `ProbSharpeRatio` [page].
- Maturity note: package metadata (PyPI JSON API) read 2026-11-10 [page]; none of these packages were run or tested.

**Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) (multiple-testing correction for SR)**
- (b) Bailey and Lopez de Prado, "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio: Correcting for Selection Bias, Backtest Overfitting and Non-Normality", Journal of Portfolio Management 40(5), 2014, pp. 94-107. SSRN https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551 [snippet]. Working-paper PDF read from the fetch cache of http://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/deflated-sharpe.pdf (July 31, 2014 version) [page]. Formulas were partly garbled by text extraction (Greek symbols lost), so formula symbols below are checked against the paper's Python snippet and wording.
- (a) Expected maximum SR over N independent zero-skill trials: E[max SR] ~ sqrt(V[SR_n]) * ( (1 - gamma_E) * Phi^-1(1 - 1/N) + gamma_E * Phi^-1(1 - 1/(N*e)) ), gamma_E = 0.5772156649 (Euler-Mascheroni). V[SR_n] = variance of the SR estimates across the N trials. [page: the paper's Python function `getExpMaxSR` does exactly this]
- DSR = PSR evaluated with the benchmark SR* set to that expected maximum. The text calls DSR "a PSR where the rejection threshold is adjusted to reflect the multiplicity of trials". [page]
- Inputs the paper says an investor should ask for: number of independent trials N, variance of backtest SRs, sample length T, skewness and kurtosis. [page]
- Worked example in paper: SR 2.5 annualised over 5 years (T = 1250 daily) is rejected at 95% because of N; the stated PSR/DSR was about 0.90 at the given N and would have been 0.9505 with N = 46 trials. [page]
- (c) Assumes the trial SRs are about normal with common mean and variance, trials independent. If M trials are correlated, use the "implied independent trials" N < M via average correlation (Appendix 3) or an entropy method; the paper says average-correlation estimates become unreliable when M exceeds T. [page]
- (d) 95% confidence is the paper's cut-off. [page] 20 independent trials give one false positive on average at 95% (the paper's "say 20 times for 95%" remark). [page]
- (e) Python: see jsharpe/sharpebench/deflated-sharpe above; the paper's appendix has only `getExpMaxSR` code. skfolio has no DSR in the README [unconfirmed].

## 2. t-statistics with overlapping or autocorrelated observations

- Why a naive t-stat is inflated: when h-period returns are sampled every period, adjacent observations share h-1 periods, so errors are serially correlated (MA(h-1) under the null of rational expectations). OLS point estimates stay consistent but OLS standard errors are wrong. [snippet: summary of Hansen-Hodrick 1980 via https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/1986/8603/econometric-methodology.html and search results]
- With persistent predictors and overlapping sums of returns the regression is "akin to a spurious regression"; conventional t-stats have rejection rates well above nominal. [page: Wei and Wright 2009, FEDS 2009-27, https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2009/200927/200927pap.pdf ]
- Hansen-Hodrick (b): Lars Peter Hansen and Robert J. Hodrick, "Forward Exchange Rates as Optimal Predictors of Future Spot Rates: An Econometric Analysis", Journal of Political Economy 88(5), 1980, pp. 829-853. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpolec:v:88:y:1980:i:5:p:829-53 [snippet]. Method: OLS point estimates with a covariance built from residual autocovariances up to lag h-1 (follows Hansen 1979). [snippet]. Exact formula: [unconfirmed].
- Newey-West (b): Newey and West, Econometrica 55(3), 1987, pp. 703-708 (reference list [page] in Wei-Wright). Lag rules seen:
  - T^(1/4): "a common choice" per Wikipedia. [page: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newey%E2%80%93West_estimator ]
  - 0.75*T^(1/3) (Stock-Watson textbook, from Andrews 1991) and 4*(T/100)^(2/9) or T^(1/4) (Wooldridge); both give 4 at T = 100. [snippet: search summary of bookdown.org/machar1991/ITER/15-4-hac-standard-errors.html and Stock lecture notes https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/stock/files/aea_2015_lecture4_har_rev.pdf ]
  - Lazarus, Lewis, Stock, Watson (2018, Journal of Business & Economic Statistics, "HAR Inference: Recommendations for Practice") recommend S = 1.3*sqrt(T) with fixed-b critical values for Newey-West tests. [snippet: PDF fetch returned 403; https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/stock/files/lazarus_lewis_stock_watson_har_inference_recommendations_for_practice_jbes_2018.pdf ]
  - Newey-West (1994, Review of Economic Studies 61(4), 631-653) "Automatic Lag Selection in Covariance Matrix Estimation": data-driven lag choice; their Monte Carlo shows tolerable performance with size distortions. [snippet: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:restud:v:61:y:1994:i:4:p:631-653. ] The 4*(T/100)^(2/9) formula was NOT confirmed as being in that paper.
  - Convention differs: some references use floor, some ceiling. [snippet]
- Rule for overlapping h-day returns: set the lag to at least h-1 (Wei-Wright use lag truncation h for Newey-West). [page: Wei and Wright 2009, simulation section]
- Failure evidence: in Wei-Wright's Monte Carlo (T = 500 monthly points, 95% nominal), Newey-West confidence intervals cover only about 80-85% at h = 12 and about 70% at h = 48; Hansen-Hodrick intervals behave very similarly. [page]
- Hodrick (1992) alternative: regress one-period return on the average of the predictor over the previous h periods (a "reverse regression"); standard errors "1B" are valid only under the null of no predictability. (Hodrick, Review of Financial Studies 5, 357-386, 1992). [page: Wei-Wright]
- Rescaling alternative: divide the OLS t-stat by sqrt(h) (Valkanov-style); one source warns this can overestimate the SE if the predictor is only weakly dependent. [snippet]
- Panel-NW as used in Petersen 2009: only lags within a cluster; with a fixed firm effect NW removes only about 60% of the bias even at maximum lag. [page: Petersen NBER w11280]
- (e) Python: statsmodels `OLS(...).fit(cov_type='HAC', cov_kwds={'maxlags': L})`; `get_robustcov_results` has `maxlags` (required, no rule given), `kernel` 'bartlett' (default) or 'uniform', `use_correction`; also 'hac-groupsum' (Driscoll-Kraay for panels) and 'cluster'. [page: https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/generated/statsmodels.regression.linear_model.RegressionResults.get_robustcov_results.html ] statsmodels 0.15.0 (2026-08-27, BSD-3) [page: PyPI JSON].
- For a signal held several days: the number of independent observations is about T/h, not T; this is my arithmetic, not a source claim.

## 3. Information coefficient (IC), rank IC, ICIR

- (a) IC = cross-sectional correlation between the forecast and the realised next-period return, one value per date. Rank IC uses Spearman rank correlation. ICIR (IC information ratio) = mean(IC) / std(IC) over dates. [page: Qlib code https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/qlib/main/qlib/contrib/eva/alpha.py ; Alphalens plotting source https://raw.githubusercontent.com/stefan-jansen/alphalens-reloaded/main/src/alphalens/plotting.py ]
- Qlib `calc_ic(pred, label, date_col, dropna)` returns Pearson IC and Spearman RankIC per date; `calc_long_short_return(pred, label, quantile=0.2)` gives the half-spread of top vs bottom quantile; `calc_long_short_prec` gives long/short precision. [page]
- Qlib report doc: IC and Rank IC graphs are daily series; long-short return is Group1 (top 20%) minus Group5; ICIR and any "good value" guidance NOT on that page. [page: https://qlib.readthedocs.io/en/latest/component/report.html ]
- Alphalens information table columns: IC Mean, IC Std., Risk-Adjusted IC (= mean/std), t-stat(IC) and p-value(IC) from `scipy.stats.ttest_1samp` against zero, IC Skew, IC Kurtosis. Also factor rank autocorrelation and quantile turnover plots/tables. [page: plotting.py source]. The README and docs home give no thresholds. [page: https://github.com/stefan-jansen/alphalens-reloaded ; https://alphalens.ml4trading.io/ ]
- Naive t-test note: ttest_1samp on IC treats the dates as independent; the IC series of an h-day-horizon signal is overlapping when sampled daily. [my inference from item 2, not a source claim]
- Fundamental law: IR ~ IC * sqrt(breadth), breadth = number of independent bets per year; Grinold (1989), Journal of Portfolio Management 15(3) [reference only]; Grinold and Kahn, Active Portfolio Management, 2nd ed., 2000. Seen only in secondary text: https://www.omscs-notes.com/machine-learning-trading/fundamental-law-active-portfolio-management/ , https://www.robeco.com/en-int/insights/2018/04/fundamental-law-of-active-management-shows-way-to-higher-information-ratio [snippet; Robeco page opened [page]: states the idea in words only, no IC numbers].

- Qlib / Alphalens / Numerai thresholds: none stated. ## 4. Portfolio sorts, Fama-MacBeth, Shanken, Petersen

**Portfolio sorts**
- What it tests: sort stocks each period on the signal into quantiles; the top-minus-bottom return spread (long-short) tested with a time-series t-stat of its mean. [background, no single page opened for this definition]. Qlib's `calc_long_short_return` and Alphalens quantile returns compute the spread. [page]
- (b) Cattaneo, Crump, Farrell, Schaumburg, "Characteristic-Sorted Portfolios: Estimation and Inference", Review of Economics and Statistics 102(3), 2020, pp. 531-551. https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.03584 [snippet]. Claims: sorting is a nonparametric estimator, number of portfolios is a tuning parameter, valid inference given, and the MSE-optimal number of portfolios can be much larger than the usual 5 or 10. [snippet]
- Transaction-cost view: Novy-Marx and Velikov, "A Taxonomy of Anomalies and Their Trading Costs" (NBER w20721). See item 9. [page]
- Harvey, Liu, Zhu: new factor needs t-ratio above 3.0 given the amount of data mining; they present Bonferroni and Holm (control family-wise error) and BHY (controls false discovery rate); in their toy table at a 5% level the Bonferroni cut-off is a p-value of 0.05%, Holm 0.60% for the lowest-ranked case shown. [page: https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592.pdf ; RFS 29(1), 5-68, 2016: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:29:y:2016:i:1:p:5-68.] [snippet for RFS citation]

**Fama-MacBeth**
- (a) Each period run a cross-sectional regression of returns on characteristics (or betas); the premium estimate = time average of the T period slopes; SE = std of period slopes / sqrt(T) (t with T-1 d.f.). [page: linearmodels doc https://bashtage.github.io/linearmodels/panel/panel/linearmodels.panel.model.FamaMacBeth.html ; Petersen paper section C]
- (b) Fama and MacBeth, "Risk, Return, and Equilibrium: Empirical Tests", Journal of Political Economy 81(3), 1973, pp. 607-636. [page: reference as listed in linearmodels doc]
- (c) Assumes slopes are serially uncorrelated across periods; it handles cross-sectional correlation within a date (time effect), not serial correlation of the same firm's residuals (firm effect). [page: Petersen NBER w11280, https://www.nber.org/papers/w11280.pdf ]
- Petersen finding: with a firm effect, Fama-MacBeth SEs are biased downward, by more than OLS (e.g., 74% vs 60% bias in his simulation with 75% firm effect); with only a time effect, Fama-MacBeth SEs are unbiased; clustered (Rogers) SEs are unbiased with a firm effect; Newey-West adjusted for panel is slightly biased. [page]
- Petersen: ad hoc inflation of FM SEs (Fama-French 2002 used a factor 2.5) and first-order autocorrelation corrections still leave bias. [page]
- Survey in paper: of 207 papers (JF, JFE, RFS, 2001-2004), 29% used Fama-MacBeth, 23% clustered SEs. [page; the 29% attached to FM sentence as read]
- Number of clusters needed: the paper says clustered SEs are consistent as the number of clusters grows; no numeric rule on the page read. A "50+" rule of thumb is [unconfirmed].
- (b) Petersen, "Estimating Standard Errors in Finance Panel Data Sets: Comparing Approaches", Review of Financial Studies 22(1), 2009, pp. 435-480. NBER w11280 (free): https://www.nber.org/papers/w11280.pdf [page]; journal https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/22/1/435/1585940 [snippet].
- Shanken (b): Jay Shanken, "On the Estimation of Beta-Pricing Models", Review of Financial Studies 5(1), 1992, pp. 1-33. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:5:y:1992:i:1:p:1-33 [snippet]. The abstract says traditional inference overstates the precision of price-of-risk estimates; an asymptotically valid correction is derived; serial correlation of market-wide factors also discussed. Source of the problem: first-pass betas are estimated with error (errors-in-variables). [snippet]
- Shanken formula: [unconfirmed] (not seen). Kan-Zhang style evidence: the unadjusted and adjusted SEs both overstate true SEs in finite samples [snippet: https://www-2.rotman.utoronto.ca/~kan/papers/csr2.pdf not opened].
- (e) Python: linearmodels `FamaMacBeth(...).fit(cov_type, debiased=True)` (linearmodels 7.1, 2026-10-16, NCSA license); `debiased=True` uses t with T-1 d.f.; the docs say the joint F-test is Hotelling T-squared (`f_statistic_robust`) and can over-reject when T is small with a kernel covariance. The docs do not mention Newey-West by name. [page] linearmodels `LinearFactorModel` (2-step risk-premia estimator) has a J-statistic; v7.1 changed J computation (example J fell from 100.6 to 41.6); no Shanken adjustment mentioned on the page. [page: https://bashtage.github.io/linearmodels/asset-pricing/asset-pricing/linearmodels.asset_pricing.model.LinearFactorModel.html ]

## 5. Alpha against factor models

- (a) Regress strategy excess returns on factor returns: r_p,t - r_f,t = alpha + b1*MKT_t + b2*SMB_t + b3*HML_t (+ MOM, RMW, CMA) + e_t. alpha = intercept; test t(alpha) with HAC SEs. Fama-French (1993, JFE 33; 2015, JFE 116). [background; not opened this session]. linearmodels `TradedFactorModel` does exactly this regression for traded factors and gives zero-alpha tests. [page: https://bashtage.github.io/linearmodels/asset-pricing/asset-pricing/linearmodels.asset_pricing.model.TradedFactorModel.html ]

## 6. Event study for news signals

- (b) A. Craig MacKinlay, "Event Studies in Economics and Finance", Journal of Economic Literature 35(1), March 1997, pp. 13-39. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:jeclit:v:35:y:1997:i:1:p:13-39 [snippet]. A scanned copy at https://www.bu.edu/econ/files/2011/01/MacKinlay-1996-Event-Studies-in-Economics-and-Finance.pdf was fetched but had no text layer (image-only; could not read). [unconfirmed content]
- Lecture notes based on Campbell, Lo, MacKinlay (1997, ch. 4) [page: https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/sapienza/ftp/Ph.D.Teaching/ECF/EmpCF_Lecture5.pdf ]: steps = define event, event window, estimation window; the event window should be short relative to the estimation window; abnormal return = actual return minus return predicted by the market model fitted on the estimation window; when event windows of different firms overlap, covariances are not zero and the aggregate variance changes; two remedies: aggregate into a calendar-time portfolio, or a multivariate regression with event dummies (poor finite-sample properties, low power). [page]
- Estimation window length: the lecture notes list past studies using different windows; no single rule on the page. Earlier notes: about 120 days typical (Wikipedia, opened previously). [page for lecture; earlier note for 120]
- (a) Definitions on eventstudytools.com: AR = R - E(R) with E(R) from market model, CAPM, FF3/FF5, Carhart or constant mean; CAR_i = sum of AR_i,t over the window; naive variance S2_CAR = L2 * S2_AR, which the page says understates the true CAR variance. [page: https://www.eventstudytools.com/methodology , https://www.eventstudytools.com/significance-tests ]
- Test statistics with formulas as given on https://www.eventstudytools.com/significance-tests [page]:
  - Cross-sectional t: t = sqrt(N) * AAR / S_AAR, t(N-1). Assumes independent, same-variance, about-normal abnormal returns; over-rejects under event-date clustering.
  - Patell: standardise each AR by its forecast-error-adjusted SD, z = ASAR / sqrt(sum (M_i - K)/(M_i - K - 2)). Sensitive to event-induced variance and cross-correlation.
  - BMP (Boehmer-Musumeci-Poulsen): cross-sectional t-test on the Patell standardised ARs; t(N-1); allows variance to rise on the event day; does not correct clustering.
  - Kolari-Pynnonen adjusted: multiply BMP t (or Patell z) by sqrt( (1 - rbar) / (1 + (N-1)*rbar) ), rbar = average pairwise correlation of estimation-window abnormal returns; costs some power; one rbar assumes a roughly constant correlation.
  - Corrado rank test: non-parametric; weaker on multi-day windows. Generalised sign (Cowan): compares count of positive ARs to estimation-window positive share; ignores magnitude.
- BMP (b): Boehmer, Musumeci, Poulsen, "Event-study methodology under conditions of event-induced variance", Journal of Financial Economics 30(2), 1991, pp. 253-272. Abstract: when an event raises variance even a little, commonly used tests reject a true zero mean too often; a simple adjustment to cross-sectional techniques fixes the size and keeps power. [snippet: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:30:y:1991:i:2:p:253-272 ; PDF at https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/lkcsb_research/article/5665/viewcontent/event_variance_pv_1991.pdf returned only a fragment]
- Kolari-Pynnonen (b): "Event Study Testing with Cross-sectional Correlation of Abnormal Returns", Review of Financial Studies 23(11), 2010, pp. 3996-4025. https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1830364 [snippet]. Stated finding: with event-date clustering, even low cross-correlation seriously over-rejects zero average abnormal return; the adjusted BMP t-statistic works for multi-day CARs. [snippet]
- (e) Python: `eventstudy` (PyPI 0.1a12, 2021-04-03, GPL-3.0; https://github.com/LemaireJean-Baptiste/eventstudy ; README lists no models/tests; 29 commits, dates not shown) [page]. `event-study-toolkit` 1.0.0 (2023-08-18) [page: PyPI JSON metadata only; content not opened]. Neither looks actively maintained (last PyPI releases 2021 and 2023) [page: dates]. R `EventStudy` package has `KolariPynnonenTest` [snippet: https://rdrr.io/cran/EventStudy/man/KolariPynnonenTest.html]. No maintained Python Kolari-Pynnonen function found.
- Assumption/failure summary: market model needs an estimation window free of the event; clustered events (same date) break independence (use K-P or calendar-time); thin trading and event-induced variance break the plain t; these are as listed on eventstudytools. [page]
- Thresholds: none numeric in sources beyond conventional 5%.

## 7. Resampling tests

**Stationary bootstrap / block bootstrap**
- (b) Politis and Romano, "The Stationary Bootstrap", Journal of the American Statistical Association 89(428), 1994, pp. 1303-1313, DOI 10.1080/01621459.1994.10476870. [snippet: search summary, https://gnosis.library.ucy.ac.cy/handle/7/57533 ]. Resamples blocks of random length (geometric distribution); resampled series are stationary, unlike the fixed-block (moving blocks) scheme. [snippet]
- (a) arch: `StationaryBootstrap(block_size)` uses exponentially distributed block lengths with mean block_size; `CircularBlockBootstrap` fixed blocks with wrap-around; `MovingBlockBootstrap` is "not recommended" because edge observations are under-sampled. [page: https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/bootstrap/timeseries-bootstraps.html ]
- Block length: `arch.bootstrap.optimal_block_length(x)` returns `b_sb` (stationary) and `b_cb` (circular), from Politis and White (2004, Econometric Reviews 23(1), 53-70) with the Patton, Politis, White (2009, Econometric Reviews 28(4), 372-375) correction; block length b = (2*g^2/d * n)^(1/3). [page: https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/bootstrap/generated/arch.bootstrap.optimal_block_length.html ]
- (c) Controls for serial dependence in the data (keeps autocorrelation inside blocks); assumes stationarity and weak dependence; with a single short series the result depends on the block length. [snippet/background; arch SPA docs default block_size to sqrt(T) if omitted [page]]
- arch version 8.0.0, 2025-10-21, NCSA [page: PyPI JSON]; scipy 1.18.1 `scipy.stats.bootstrap` (IID only) [unconfirmed: not opened].

**Data-snooping tests (best of many rules)**
- White (2000), "A Reality Check for Data Snooping", Econometrica 68(5), 1097-1126. Tests the null that the best model found in a specification search has no predictive superiority over a benchmark, using the bootstrap. [snippet: https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2000/09/01/reality-check-data-snooping ]

**Monte Carlo permutation / randomised-entry tests**
- Aronson method as described by Adaptrade: randomly re-pair the strategy's daily positions with daily price changes (permute without replacement); null = strategy worthless; for many candidate strategies, record the best statistic per permutation to build the best-of-N distribution; p-value = share of that distribution at or above the observed value; typical 5% level. ## 8. Hit rate / directional accuracy; calibration

- Binomial test: H0: probability of a correct sign = p (default 0.5). Python `scipy.stats.binomtest(k, n, p=0.5, alternative='two-sided'|'greater'|'less')`, result has `proportion_ci(confidence_level=0.95, method='exact')`. [page: https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.binomtest.html ] Assumes independent trials; with overlapping h-day outcomes the trials are not independent (my inference).
- Pesaran-Timmermann (b): M. Hashem Pesaran and Allan Timmermann, "A Simple Nonparametric Test of Predictive Performance", Journal of Business & Economic Statistics 10(4), 1992, pp. 461-465. (Search summary gave page range 461-465; some listings give 561-65.) https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bes:jnlbes:v:10:y:1992:i:4:p:561-65 [snippet]
- (a) p_hat = share of periods where sign(forecast) = sign(actual). p_star = py*pz + (1-py)*(1-pz), py = share of positive actuals, pz = share of positive forecasts. S = (p_hat - p_star) / sqrt(v_hat - w_hat), v_hat = p_star*(1-p_star)/n, w_hat = [ (2py-1)^2 * pz*(1-pz) + (2pz-1)^2 * py*(1-py) ] / n. Asymptotically N(0,1) under no directional skill. [page: https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/generated/statsmodels.stats.diagnostic.pesaran_timmermann.html ]
- (c) Why not a plain binomial test against 50%: the benchmark p_star accounts for the base rate of ups (a strategy that always says "up" in a rising market scores high but gets S undefined; the statistic is undefined if all actual or all predicted signs are identical). [snippet for the undefined case; the base-rate point follows from the formula]
- (e) Python: `statsmodels.stats.diagnostic.pesaran_timmermann(actual, predicted, alternative='two-sided')` returns statistic and pvalue. [page] R: rugarch `DACTest`. [snippet]
- Calibration: scikit-learn `calibration_curve(y_true, y_prob, n_bins=5, strategy='uniform'|'quantile')` returns observed fraction positive vs mean predicted probability per bin; `CalibrationDisplay.from_estimator`; the doc recommends looking at the histogram of bin counts; few bins give a stable but coarse curve, many bins are noisy. [page: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html ; parameter list there was from the model's own recall, not the page -> signature [unconfirmed]]
- Brier score: BS = mean( (p_i - y_i)^2 ), lower is better. scikit-learn page says Brier and log loss are strictly proper scoring rules, but Brier mixes calibration, discrimination (resolution) and inherent randomness (Murphy decomposition), so a lower Brier does not always mean better calibration. [page: scikit-learn calibration page; the formula itself was supplied by the tool, not printed on the page -> formula [snippet]]
- Brier (b): Glenn W. Brier, "Verification of Forecasts Expressed in Terms of Probability", Monthly Weather Review 78(1), 1950, pp. 1-3, DOI 10.1175/1520-0493(1950)078<0001:VOFEIT>2.0.CO;2 [snippet: DOI via a Wikipedia citation in a search result].
- Rule of thumb from a source: none found for a "good" hit rate or Brier score for stock direction. [unconfirmed]

## 9. Economic significance

**Turnover and costs**
- (b) Novy-Marx and Velikov, "A Taxonomy of Anomalies and Their Trading Costs", NBER w20721. https://www.nber.org/papers/w20721 [page, abstract]; full PDF read via https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20721/w20721.pdf [page]. Journal: Review of Financial Studies 29(1), 2016 [unconfirmed: from memory].
- Findings stated: most anomalies with one-sided monthly turnover below 50% still give statistically significant net spreads when designed to limit costs; few with higher turnover do. A buy/hold spread (keep holding stocks you would not newly buy) is the single most effective simple cost-mitigation step. [page]
- Round-trip cost for typical value-weighted strategies averages over 50 bps (US); costs reduce a realised spread by more than 1% of monthly one-sided turnover (e.g., 20% monthly one-sided turnover cuts the spread by at least 20 bps per month); equal-weighted strategies cost two to three times as much. [page] Many strategies stay significant at t > 2 after costs but far fewer at t > 3. [page]
- Break-even transaction cost: the per-trade cost at which net alpha is zero = gross spread / turnover (my definition; NMV report net returns and cost estimates but I did not find a "break-even cost" table on the pages read). [unconfirmed as a source formula]
- HK costs: see research2 (stamp duty 0.1% each side, etc.). [page, earlier]

**Alpha decay / half-life**
- McLean and Pontiff, "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?", Journal of Finance 71(1), 2016, pp. 5-32, DOI 10.1111/jofi.12365.
